Bayesian Optimization with Gaussian Process Surrogates

GP posteriors, covariance functions, acquisition strategies

The foundations of Bayesian optimization trace back to work on efficient global optimization (EGO) (Jones et al. 1998), which introduced expected improvement as an acquisition function for response surface optimization. The theoretical framework using Gaussian processes draws heavily from the comprehensive treatment in (Rasmussen and Williams 2006).

Modern tutorials (Brochu et al. 2010; Frazier 2018) provide accessible introductions to the field, while (Shahriari et al. 2016) offers an extensive review covering methodological advances and applications. The GP-UCB algorithm (Srinivas et al. 2012) established regret bounds connecting Bayesian optimization to the bandit literature.

Practical Bayesian optimization for machine learning hyperparameter tuning was demonstrated by (Snoek et al. 2012), showing that careful GP prior design leads to state-of-the-art performance. High-dimensional extensions include random embeddings (Wang et al. 2016), local trust regions (Eriksson et al. 2019), and sparse axis-aligned subspaces (Eriksson and Jankowiak 2021).

Information-theoretic acquisition functions (Hernández-Lobato et al. 2014) provide principled approaches to exploration, while portfolio methods (Brochu et al. 2011) combine multiple acquisition strategies. Multi-fidelity methods (Kandasamy et al. 2017; Li et al. 2020; Swersky et al. 2014) leverage cheap approximations to accelerate optimization. Transfer learning (Feurer et al. 2022; Swersky et al. 2013) enables knowledge transfer across related optimization tasks.

Scalable Gaussian process inference (Snelson and Ghahramani 2006; Titsias 2009; Matthews et al. 2016) underpins practical BO implementations, with GPyTorch (Gardner et al. 2018) providing efficient GPU-accelerated computation. Applications span neural architecture search (Kandasamy et al. 2018; Falkner et al. 2018), biological sequence design (Stanton et al. 2022), and safe exploration (Sui et al. 2015).

Read as a list of names that is a heap. It becomes a chapter once one question is put to every row: where does the GP posterior stop paying for itself, and what does this method give up to buy that place back? EGO and GP-UCB pay nothing and work only where the posterior is already cheap and accurate, in low dimension with few observations. Sparse and variational GPs give up exactness of the posterior to survive n∼104n \sim 10^4. REMBO, TuRBO and SAASBO give up the assumption that the posterior is a single global object, respectively by projecting, by localising, and by zeroing most coordinates. Multi-fidelity and freeze-thaw methods give up the requirement that every evaluation be the true objective. Transfer methods give up the requirement that every observation come from the current task. Safe and constrained BO give up free access to the design space. The table below records that trade for each row: Regime is where the posterior still pays, Headline result is what the trade bought.
